\subsection{Comparison with Model-Predictive Control: a Lookahead Ablation}\label{EV_MPC}
This subsection is best read not as a stronger-baseline comparison but as a
controlled ablation of planning horizon: the three window tiers hold
everything else fixed -- information, constraints, fairness memory, execution
physics -- and vary only how far ahead the optimizer plans. We benchmark
against a model-predictive controller built to be the strongest
fair opponent: a windowed MILP over $\Delta{=}30$\,s macro-slots that receives
the same deterministic schedules the proposed scheduler can compute offline
(sunlit, ISL, eclipse forecast) and the same causal state, with future
arrivals entering only through the rate $\lambda$; it carries the same
cross-window fairness memory (window objective $\max U$ s.t.\ $U \le D_i +
\sum_{\text{window}} q_i$ with $D_i$ the negative of the $Q^U_i$ content); it
is exempt from the eclipse-reserve guard, with battery safety left to its own
optimization through a terminal condition (end-of-window charge must cover the
platform to the next sunrise) and a planning-layer relaxation of the battery
floor whose penalty (10 quality units per Joule) exceeds any action's
quality-per-Joule by $43\times$. The relaxation exists only in the planning
layer: at execution every MPC action passes the same shared feasibility
oracle and the same safe-mode blackout accounting as every baseline. Plans
re-solve every $R{=}60$\,s (CBC, solved to 0.5\% MIP gap) under two declared
regimes: \emph{real-time}, where the solve is capped at $R$ and the new plan
takes effect only after the measured solve wall-clock has elapsed (the
previous plan keeps executing meanwhile), and \emph{oracle}, where solve
time is not charged to the simulation clock, an upper bound. Windows $W \in
\{\text{half-eclipse } 1035\,\mathrm{s}, \text{eclipse } 2070\,\mathrm{s},
\text{orbit } 5736\,\mathrm{s}\}$; 10 seeds throughout.

% ---- flip narrative: the fairness premium belongs to orbit-scale lookahead ----
The window sweep turns the comparison from a verdict into a mechanism
(Table~\ref{tab_mpc}). With a half-eclipse window the MILP, holding full SoC
visibility, guard exemption, and the full per-window optimization machinery,
does \emph{not} outperform the per-slot scheduler (min-fill 0.152 versus
0.165); with an eclipse-length window it ties (0.170); only when the window
covers the full sunlit--eclipse cycle does a fairness premium open (0.269,
with total quality 3999 versus 2151). Notably, the premium costs no
availability: MPC's downtime equals the proposed scheduler's at every tier
(1.59\%, the common start-of-trace transient) -- the orbit-tier fairness was
available \emph{inside the same safety envelope}, and what unlocks it is
orbit-scale lookahead, not SoC visibility, guard exemption, or optimization
power at matched horizons. Its price is measured by the same experiment:
per-decision compute $4.2\times10^{3}$--$4.5\times10^{4}\times$ the proposed
scheduler's $27\,\mu$s per-slot rule, a solve-time distribution whose
$\tau{=}1$\,s exceedance grows from 0\% (half) through 3.5\% (eclipse) to
36\% (orbit), and a reliance on a MILP solver on-board. The real-time and
oracle regimes coincide throughout (the 60\,s incumbent is already
near-optimal; 0 incumbent failures; no solve with MIP gap above 50\%), so
the premium is real rather than an artifact of uncharged solve time; by the
pre-registered criterion every tier remains usable at the real-time budget
on the workstation.

% ---- amortization caliber sentence ----
Two compute columns answer two different questions: amortized per executed
slot the ratio shrinks mechanically with $W$ (one solve per 60 slots against
a rule run every slot, $69\times$ at $W{=}$half), which measures the average
compute bill; the per-decision figure and the $\tau$-exceedance fraction
measure whether a decision is available \emph{on time}, and on-board
scheduling is the latter problem -- a missed replanning cycle is a real
failure mode, an average bill is not.

% ---- phase mechanism (orbit only; eclipse reported plainly, no mechanism) ----
For the orbit tier the hard instances cluster at eclipse boundaries (median
distance 125 slots versus 355 for all solves): the solves are slowest
precisely when timely replanning matters most. The eclipse tier shows no
clear clustering (282 versus 356, inside the pre-registered ambiguity) and
is reported without a mechanism claim.

% ---- Jetson paragraph (paired re-solve, pre-registered sampling) ----
On the target device (Jetson Orin NX) we re-solve, per tier, ten windows
drawn by a pre-registered rule (eight octile-median instances plus the two
hardest) and pair them with unloaded workstation re-solves of the identical
instances, both at the real-time budget (60\,s cap, 0.5\% gap). The verdict
is carried by the body of the distribution: on the device the orbit tier
exceeds $\tau$ on \emph{all ten} instances (fastest 1.59\,s, mean 6.7\,s) --
the only window that collects the fairness premium is never per-slot
real-time on-board -- while half crosses on 0/10 and eclipse on 2/10. The
distribution body transfers at ${\sim}3.2\times$ (device/workstation, the
four easiest orbit instances 3.2--3.3$\times$); per-instance hardness in the
tail does not transfer (a mid-stratum orbit instance grows from 0.78\,s on
the workstation to 18.6\,s on the device, while the workstation's
10.3\,s instance collapses to 1.6\,s), and the same tail reshuffled when
the quality mapping was recalibrated from stylized to measured values.
Branch-and-bound hardness is thus fragile to both architecture and
objective coefficients: which windows will be slow on-board cannot be
predicted from ground profiling, which is itself a deployment liability for
on-board MILP and the reason the $\tau$-exceedance fraction, not any
per-instance figure, is the declared real-time criterion.
