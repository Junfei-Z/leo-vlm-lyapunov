\section{Performance Evaluation}\label{EVAL}
We evaluate the framework in three signposted parts so the reader can place each
figure. We first \emph{compare} the proposed scheduler against four baselines on the
constellation, isolating where it leads and where it only trades off. We then
\emph{validate} the theoretical guarantees against a certified offline optimum and the
stability conditions. We finally \emph{stress-test} robustness under intermittent
inter-satellite links and varying eclipse. A comparison figure always shows all four
baselines; a validation or stress-test figure reports the proposed scheduler against the
relevant theoretical reference.

\subsection{Hardware Calibration and the Quality--Energy Landscape}\label{EV_CALIB}
We profile per-configuration inference cost on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX (16\,GB, JetPack~6,
\texttt{llama.cpp} CUDA backend, \texttt{Q4\_K\_M} weights, fixed 15\,W power mode). The task is
45-class remote-sensing scene classification on RESISC45~\cite{Cheng2017RESISC45}. Latency is
timed per image; power is sampled every 50\,ms with \texttt{tegrastats}; energy integrates power
over the inference window; the peak bound $\hat{P}_{im}^{\text{CP}}$ is the 95th percentile.

We profiled seven vision-language models spanning four families and 2--8B parameters. Two
findings drive the rest of the evaluation. First, accuracy is not monotone in model size:
several smaller or newer models outscore larger ones (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}), because
architecture and model generation matter as much as parameter count. A configuration's quality
therefore cannot be predicted from its size and must be measured; the scheduler reasons over the
measured operating points, and its action set is the Pareto-efficient subset of profiled
configurations (Table~\ref{tab_calib}). Second, the deployment target is not the 16\,GB profiling
host but a typical sensing satellite with an $\sim$8\,GB edge module whose memory is shared with
the on-board detection pipeline, leaving only a few gigabytes for inference. Under this budget any
configuration of 4B parameters or more cannot run standalone and must be split across two
satellites; the highest-quality reachable model, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, is exactly such a split-only
configuration, which is what makes the two-stage pipeline necessary rather than optional.

\subsection{Simulation Setup}\label{EV_SETUP}
Each slot is $\tau=1$\,s. One orbit spans 5700 slots (3420 sunlit, 2280 eclipse), matching the
$60\%/40\%$ profile of a 550\,km orbit; we run three orbits (17{,}100 slots). The constellation has
four computing satellites with staggered eclipse phases and six sensing sources. On-board inference
is a secondary, opportunistic load: the platform's sensing, communication, and attitude-control
subsystems consume most of the power and energy budget. We therefore size the solar panel so that
one orbit's harvest balances the inference demand (a small 2\,W panel), and we model a platform
baseline draw $P_{\text{base}}{=}9$\,W that shares the satellite's $P^{\text{cap}}{=}15$\,W power
bus with inference. The per-satellite memory budget admits the 2B and 3B configurations standalone,
while the 7B model runs only as a two-stage split across two ISL-connected satellites. The
calibrated values of Table~\ref{tab_calib} populate every cost term. We report five quantities per
policy: peak-power violations, battery-blackout downtime, service rate, the minimum quality-fill
ratio across sources (the empirical max-min objective $\min_i \bar{q}_i$), and cumulative delivered
quality.

\subsection{Benchmark Solutions}\label{EV_BASE}
We compare against four online baselines, each relaxing one or more structural constraints so the
comparison isolates the value of respecting them. Greedy-Q always selects the highest-quality
standalone configuration; it ignores energy sustainability, so under a tight budget it drains the
eclipse-side battery, and because it omits the peak check it can exceed the power bus. Greedy-E
always selects the cheapest configuration; it stays energy-safe but never reaches the higher tiers.
Random selects a feasible standalone configuration uniformly at random. Static routes to the
highest-battery satellite and runs a single fixed standalone configuration. None of the four can
orchestrate the two-satellite split, so all are capped at the 3B standalone tier. We treat the
offline MILP solely as a clairvoyant upper bound for the optimality-gap study
(Section~\ref{EV_GAP}), not as a competing policy: it is non-causal, requiring full future
knowledge of solar arrivals, channels, and eclipse, so comparing it on equal footing with causal
online schedulers would be unfair, and it is intractable at constellation scale.

\subsection{Multi-Satellite Comparison}\label{EV_MULTI}
Table~\ref{tab_split} and Fig.~\ref{fig:split} report the four-satellite comparison with the split
pipeline. The dependable advantage is sustainability: under the realistic small-panel budget the
proposed scheduler incurs zero battery-blackout downtime, whereas Greedy-Q chases the highest tier,
drains the eclipse-side batteries, and suffers 2791 blackout slots. The proposed scheduler is also
the only policy that runs the 7B tier, executing 3529 inferences as a two-satellite split; the four
baselines are structurally capped at the 3B standalone tier. Cumulative quality is an honest
trade-off: energy-aware baselines such as Static reach a higher raw throughput (3703.6 versus
3435.6) by running a cheap fixed configuration at full duty, while the proposed scheduler spends
part of its budget on staying blackout-free and on the expensive 7B split and optimizes the max-min
objective rather than throughput. Within these constraints it keeps a competitive minimum
quality-fill (0.333) while remaining the only safe and top-tier-capable policy. We did not tune the
scenario to force a quality win.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}\label{EV_SENS}
We sweep eight scenario parameters one at a time and record all five policies at every point
(Figs.~\ref{fig:sens_safety} and~\ref{fig:sens_quality}). Two safety results are invariant. First,
the proposed scheduler incurs zero blackout at every point, while baselines black out heavily as
the solar panel shrinks: at a 1\,W panel Greedy-Q loses 11{,}092 slots and Greedy-E 7417, and the
default 2\,W panel sits in the regime where the advantage binds. Second, the per-slot shield is
decisive when the platform load tightens the power bus: as $P_{\text{base}}$ rises and the inference
headroom $P^{\text{cap}}{-}P_{\text{base}}$ falls below the configuration draws, Greedy-Q, Greedy-E,
Random, and Static blow the 15\,W bus 14{,}000--16{,}000 times, while the proposed scheduler and its
shield never violate it. The quality picture is the honest trade-off: the max-min advantage grows
with the constellation (min-fill 0.423 and 0.422 at 8 and 16 satellites, versus 0.25--0.33 for the
baselines) and under overload (at a 70\% arrival probability the proposed scheduler protects the
worst source at 0.100 while the greedy and static baselines starve it to 0.001). With only two
satellites or light load the safety margin is not needed and energy-aware baselines reach a higher
min-fill; the value of the method concentrates where the LEO setting is hard.

\subsection{Optimality Gap Against the Offline Benchmark}\label{EV_GAP}
We solve the offline MILP to certified optimality on a small but faithful instance (two satellites,
two sources, a 48-slot scaled orbit) over twelve randomized instances, each solved to a proven
optimum (mean $U^\star{=}11.14$). Fig.~\ref{fig:gap} reports the result. As $V$ grows from 1 to 500
the online minimum delivered quality rises from $0.56$ to $1.00$ of the offline optimum and the
normalized gap falls from $0.44$ to $0$, tracking the $O(1/V)$ reference of Theorem~\ref{thm_gap};
peak feasibility holds at every $V$. Fig.~\ref{fig:sim_V} shows the matching single-satellite
$[O(1/V),O(V)]$ knob: a larger $V$ raises the delivered min-quality toward the optimum at the cost of
a larger queue backlog.

\subsection{Dynamic ISL Topology}\label{EV_ISL}
To test the handoff constraint~\eqref{C_pipe_isl}, we replace the static topology with a
time-varying one of controllable availability $p_{\text{ISL}}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:isl}). As availability
drops from 1.0 to 0.3 the number of feasible 7B splits falls from 3529 to 1371, so the constraint
genuinely binds. The scheduler reroutes the blocked splits to standalone configurations; because the
split path is resource-expensive, cumulative quality and the max-min fill are essentially unchanged
(in fact slightly higher, 3610.6 versus 3435.6) and the blackout count stays at zero throughout. The
cost of intermittent connectivity is paid as fewer top-tier inferences, not as lost throughput or
lost safety.

\subsection{Stability Stress Tests}\label{EV_STAB}
Two experiments probe the guarantees directly. Fig.~\ref{fig:sim_timeseries} traces one satellite
over three orbits: the energy virtual queue rises during each eclipse and recovers in sunlight, the
battery cycles periodically, and the queues stay bounded across all orbits, the empirical signature
of the robust stability of Theorem~\ref{thm_robust}. Fig.~\ref{fig:eclipse} varies the eclipse fraction: the time-average
Lyapunov backlog grows smoothly and stays bounded with zero violations across the range, the soft
margin of the ISS condition. Fig.~\ref{fig:longhorizon} runs twenty orbits: the energy and power
queues are mean-rate stable ($Q_s^E(T)/T\to1.5\times10^{-4}$, $Q_s^P(T)/T=0$) per
Theorem~\ref{thm_stable}, while the quality-deficit queue stays bounded at the Slater boundary.
